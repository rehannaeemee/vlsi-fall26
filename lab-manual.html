Background

1. RTL-to-GDSII Flow Overview

An RTL-to-GDSII flow progressively transforms a behavioral hardware description into a physically manufacturable layout. Each stage adds information, checks correctness, and produces files required by the next stage. The flow is iterative: a timing or physical violation may require returning to an earlier stage.

This flow chart shows many of the individual stages digital designers follow in industry. However, it does not show the cyclic nature between individual stages. For example, a bug discovered in Simulation provokes RTL Design modifications. In general, problems discovered in the back end sometimes require changes in the front end. Therefore, it is imperative that you are well-versed in the mechanics of simulating your designs before designing anything.

The principal stages to pay attention to are RTL Design, Synthesis, Place Design, and Routing. The last two stages are often combined and termed Place and Route.

2. CAD Tools

Going through the design flow is quite labor-intensive and intricate. In general, computer-aided design (CAD) software tools are programs used to reduce the burden of manually performing each stage of a design flow. Electronic design automation (EDA), or electronic computer-aided design (ECAD), tools are specifically created to aid integrated-circuit design flows.

The three major CAD companies for ASIC design are Cadence, Synopsys, and Siemens. Each supplies tools for all stages of the Very Large-Scale Integration (VLSI) flow. VLSI refers to complex integrated circuits containing thousands or more transistors.

3. SkyWater 130 and Process Design Kits

SkyWater 130, or SKY130, is an open-source process design kit (PDK) for a 130 nm technology node. The details of a PDK will become clearer through later laboratories. Major foundries such as Intel, GlobalFoundries, and TSMC provide PDKs for their fabrication processes.

A PDK is a set of files used by semiconductor design tools to model a fabrication process. PDKs are commonly specific to a foundry and may be proprietary, although some PDKs are open source and publicly available. (Adapted from UC San Diego PDK guidance.)

4. Simulation

Simulation is a fundamental step in ASIC design and digital design in general. Different forms of simulation can occur at several stages of the design cycle; the two principal forms considered here are behavioral simulation, also called RTL simulation, and gate-level simulation.

4.1 Testbenches

A testbench is an HDL module used only for simulation. It instantiates the design under test (DUT), drives its inputs, and verifies its outputs.

4.2 Waveforms

A waveform file records selected signals during simulation so they can be viewed as traces after the run. Desired signals must be marked before simulation; a signal that was not recorded cannot be added to an already completed waveform. Waveforms are the primary means by which digital designers debug RTL. Use the following order:

  1. Look for obvious bugs in the RTL.
  2. If the bug is not obvious, inspect the waveform.

4.3 RTL Simulation

RTL simulation is one of the first steps used to check the functionality or behavior of an RTL design. Fixing bugs at the RTL stage makes subsequent debugging easier, and the required computing resources and runtime are much lower than for gate-level simulation.

4.4 Gate-Level Simulation

Gate-level simulation is performed after a design has been synthesized. Synthesis transforms a behavioral Verilog representation into digital logic gates, or cells, from a given library to form a netlist. Post-synthesis simulation therefore evaluates the design at the gate level.

By default, logic gates behave ideally: their outputs appear valid instantly when a new input is presented. In real silicon, gate delay varies with operating conditions such as voltage, process variation, and temperature. CAD tools calculate these delays and annotate them onto gates using a Standard Delay Format (SDF) file.

Gate-level simulations with timing annotation model signal propagation. A gate output does not change instantly when its input changes; the signal must propagate through the gate.

SDC (Synopsys Design Constraints) File

An SDC file contains timing and environmental constraints used by synthesis, static timing analysis, and place-and-route tools.

LIB (Library) File

Timing data of standard cells is provided in the Liberty format. General information common to all cells in the library. For example, operating conditions, wire load models, look-up tables. Specific information about each standard cell.

Standard Delay Format (SDF) File

SDF is an IEEE standard representation of timing data that can be used at different stages of the electronic-design process.
Note: Detailed knowledge of every SDF feature is unnecessary for this lab. Students interested in additional detail may consult this four-part SDF tutorial.

Example SDF cell definition:

(CELL
  (CELLTYPE "CLKINVX2")
  (INSTANCE adde.g768)
  (DELAY
    (ABSOLUTE
      (PORT A (::0.1))
      (IOPATH A Y (::157) (::128))
    )
  )
)

The example is a single cell definition from src/post-syn/fir.mapped.sdf. Its main fields are:

  • CELLTYPE — names the type of cell in the PDK.
  • INSTANCE — identifies the specific instance of the cell in the current design.
  • DELAY — provides timing information for the cell.
  • ABSOLUTE — indicates that the timing values are absolute.
  • PORT A — provides timing information for input A.
  • IOPATH — provides propagation timing from input A to output Y; the example cell is an inverter.

The delay format is minimum:typical:maximum. Minimum and maximum values represent different operating regions. The example specifies maximum delays of either 157 ps or 128 ps, depending on whether the data transitions from low to high or from high to low. The SDF timescale declaration determines that these values are in picoseconds.

5. What Is Synthesis?

Synthesis transforms RTL, typically written in Verilog or VHDL, into a gate-level netlist. A synthesized gate-level Verilog netlist contains cells from the target technology. For each cell, the PDK supplies information such as a transistor-level schematic with transistor sizes, a physical layout for fabrication, and timing libraries containing performance specifications.

Cadence Genus is used for synthesis in this course. The process begins with compilation and elaboration of RTL. According to IEEE Std 1800-2017, compilation reads the RTL and analyzes it for syntax and semantic errors. Elaboration expands instantiations and hierarchies, resolves parameter values, and establishes netlist connectivity. In Genus, elaboration produces a generic netlist composed of generic gates such as CDN_flop and CDN_mux2. These generic gates do not yet correspond physically to cells in the target standard-cell library.

6. Introduction to Static Timing Analysis

Static Timing Analysis (STA) validates timing performance by analyzing timing arcs for violations. Broadly, it identifies timing arcs, calculates propagation delays, and checks setup and hold requirements. Basic delay concepts can be understood by inspecting timing-library fragments.

Two important classes of delay are cell delay and net delay. In a Liberty timing model, the delay through a cell can be represented by a two-dimensional lookup table indexed by input-net transition and cell output capacitance. The timing sense describes the relationship between input and output transitions. 7. Standard Cell

A standard cell is a predefined physical layout of transistors for logic gates and storage elements such as latches or flip-flops. Standard cells have the same height, but they may differ in width. The number and size of the transistors determine the size of a standard cell. Each cell comes with timing and power information used during place and route. A cell library is the collection of standard cells used for a process and is part of the PDK.

Within the same PDK, multiple cells may implement the same logic gate using different transistor sizes. These variants allow the CAD tool to select a cell that meets power and timing specifications. Larger transistors consume more power but can drive larger currents; smaller transistors follow the opposite trend.

8. VLSI

ASICs are three-dimensional structures resembling a layer cake. At the bottom is the substrate, where the transistors lie. Above the substrate are alternating dielectric and metal layers. The number of layers is process-dependent and determined by the foundry.

Metal layers are an important concept in ASIC design because transistors are connected through them. A route must pass through the dielectric to a metal layer, travel through one or more metal layers, and then return toward the substrate to connect transistors. Connections between metal layers are called vias. Power rails are also contained in metal layers. Typically, upper layers, around M4 and above, carry global routes, while layers closer to the substrate carry local routes. Power distribution uses the higher layers, whereas routes between nearby logic gates generally use the lower layers.

9. Place and Route

Place and route actualizes a design or circuit using physical transistors. It is part of physical design, which is also called PD. Place and route—often written as PAR, P&R, or PnR—follows synthesis in both ASIC and FPGA digital-design flows. The netlist produced during synthesis expresses the entire design at the gate level using cells from the selected PDK. During place and route, a physical cell instance is created for each cell in the netlist and placed in the chip area. Routes, or traces, are then created to connect the cells as specified by the netlist.

Better placement of cells generally enables more efficient routing, which can reduce area and improve timing. Poor placement requires longer routes and often causes timing failures. This is one reason synthesis timing reports are not fully accurate for the final physical circuit. The output of place and route is a physical layout.

Place and route is the most complex and longest stage in the digital-design flow. Although its detailed algorithms are beyond the scope of this introductory material, their general strategy is important. The design is first partitioned into subdesigns so parallel processing can reduce runtime. The initial placement is then iterated until the tool finds an implementation that satisfies power, timing, area, and user constraints. Different CAD tools use different algorithms, but their flows include similar stages:

  1. Floorplanning
  2. Clock-tree synthesis
  3. Placement and routing
Note: This section provides a simple introduction. Place and route is the most critical stage in the ASIC design flow, and all of its details cannot be covered within a single lab.

9.1 Floorplanning

Floorplanning is the process of allocating area for a design, constraining how that area is used, and planning the power network. It is often the most important factor in determining the performance of a physical circuit because an intelligent floorplan can help the tool minimize design delays, especially when the available area is highly constrained.

For a standard design, the main goal is to find the smallest possible floorplan that still fulfills all performance and reliability requirements. This goal is driven by economics because chip cost is strongly influenced by area. A manufacturer may also impose geometrical constraints. Back-end design is not limited to complete chips: macro-cells that will become part of a larger system-on-chip can be designed in the same way. Such blocks may have additional restrictions; for example, particular metal layers may be reserved for system-level routing.

Floorplan constraints can be hard or soft. Hard constraints generally involve the pre-placement of macros, which can range from memory elements such as SRAM arrays to analog black boxes such as PLLs or LDOs. Soft constraints generally guide the placement of hierarchical modules—such as a datapath, controller, or FIFO—toward particular floorplan regions. Place-and-route tools generally place hierarchical modules effectively, but human guidance may occasionally be required to obtain the final amount of performance.

The die area is the total silicon area available for pads and core cells. All I/O, power, and corner pads are placed in the padframe, another level of hierarchy in which both the original design and the I/O cells are instantiated. The remaining area can be used for the chip core.

The core area is surrounded by a core power ring that distributes power around the chip. In its simplest form, the ring consists of two wide metal lines: one for VDD and one for VSS. Space must be left between the I/O region and the core to accommodate this power ring; this separation is called the I/O-to-core spacing.

The core area is filled with standard-cell rows on which the standard cells are placed. Utilization indicates the percentage of the standard-cell rows occupied by cells. Standard cells are designed so that, when placed side by side, their VDD and VSS pins can be connected by one horizontal metal line each. These relatively narrow power connections run across the core and connect to the core power rings. In high-current designs, cells near the center of the core may otherwise have weak power connections. To improve power delivery, the two uppermost metal layers are commonly used to place a power grid across the core. Vertical and horizontal power stripes in this grid connect down through the metal stack to the standard-cell power rails.

Space must also be reserved in the core area for macro-cells. Many macros require free space around them for signal connections, a block power ring, or separation from nearby standard cells. A block halo defines this reserved space.

9.2 Power Planning

The goal of power planning is to distribute power evenly to every part of the design.

The principal concerns during power routing are electromigration and IR drop. Electromigration is the movement of conductor atoms caused by electrical current; it can produce shorts, open circuits, performance degradation, or device failure. Electromigration increases with temperature and current density, so wider wires can reduce its effect. IR drop is the reduction in voltage caused by the resistance of the power wires.

A chip may require multiple power domains. The domains and the signals crossing between them are specified in the power intent.

VDD and VSS are global connections because they connect to every cell. Upper metal layers are well suited to distribute power across the chip, but standard cells require power at M1. The power-distribution hierarchy therefore includes:

  • Rings carry VDD and VSS around the chip, similar to beltways.
  • Stripes carry VDD and VSS across the chip, similar to main roads.
  • Rails carry VDD and VSS to the standard cells, similar to streets.

During power planning, the designer selects the layers used for rings, stripes, and rails and determines the pitch and spacing on each layer. Pitch is the distance between two adjacent VDD lines or two adjacent VSS lines in the same metal layer. Spacing is the minimum distance between adjacent VDD and VSS lines. Vertical connections between metal layers are called vias.

Spacing and pitch affect both power integrity and routing congestion. Poor power placement can also introduce electromagnetic effects such as crosstalk and coupling capacitance, degrading overall performance.

9.3 Placement

Placement is the process of placing the synthesized design—the structural connection of standard cells—onto the specified floorplan. Some minor cells, such as bulk-connection cells, antenna-effect prevention cells, and I/O buffers, may be placed separately at different stages of the flow; however, placement usually refers to the initial placement of standard cells.

After placement, the cells are not locked and may be moved by the tool during later optimization. Initial placement nevertheless attempts to position the cells optimally while obeying floorplan constraints. It uses complex heuristics to minimize parasitic delay in the connecting wires and timing skew between synchronous elements such as flip-flops and memories. Poor placement, or a poor floorplan aspect ratio, can cause routing congestion and may prevent the design from being routed successfully.

During placement, the tool attempts to position cells close to one another based on the connectivity graph of the netlist. In particular, it tries to keep the fan-in and fan-out cells of each element physically near each other. This process is repeated across all netlist elements with the objective of minimizing a cost function—commonly the routing cost, such as the L2 distance between connected cells—while still maintaining a target utilization (e.g., 70%). However, when the available placement area is limited, this optimization can lead to regions where cells become densely packed. Such high-density placement negatively impacts the subsequent routing stage. Since the placement area also determines the available metal routing resources above the cells, densely packed regions can demand more routing resources than are actually available. As a result, routing in these congested areas becomes significantly more complex, often leading to excessive runtime or even failure to complete. Therefore, it is crucial to analyze congestion after placement and address it early. This can be done by improving the floorplan (e.g., rearranging macros), introducing placement blockages, or adding padding to create more whitespace in highly congested regions. What makes a placement good can sometimes be a difficult question to answer. When looking at the entire IC design process a good placement is one that minimizes the used area, while keeping the design routable and delivering the highest possible operating frequency. However, evaluating these metrics is only fully possible at the very end of the design process, not in the middle where we are. So instead in the placement stage, we try to estimate said metrics.

Clock Tree

The clock net is a special net in every synchronous digital design: you already noticed in the placement & timing exercise that special timing constraints apply to the clocks of the system. In this exercise, we will explain how the clock is distributed to all the flip-flops in the design. We will motivate why the clock net requires a special distribution network on the chip and consider important parameters of clock distribution: clock insertion delay, clock skew, and clock jitter.

Our goal is to make sure that the clock signal reaches all endpoints (called leaf cells) more or less at the same time and as efficiently as possible. We define the following basic properties for a clock tree:

  • Transition time: This is the time it takes for a node to change logic level (e.g., from 0 to 1). The more load an output has to drive, the more time is required to charge this load. CMOS drivers consume additional short-circuit current during the transition; therefore, long transition times are undesirable. Furthermore, noise on signals with long transition times can result in glitching. Most libraries set an upper limit for the transition time (for the technology we are using, this is 2.5 ns for typical libraries). A tree of buffers can be inserted to reduce the transition time so that the total load is shared between the buffers. The lower the desired transition time, the more buffers are required.
  • Clock Skew: The difference between the insertion delays of different end-points. To minimize skew, a balanced buffer tree has to be built. In general, the lower the desired skew, the more buffers are required.
  • Insertion delay: The time required for the signal to travel from the driver to the endpoints. In most cases, this delay is the result of engineering a clock tree with a certain number of levels, based on our constraints on transition time and skew.

In some cases, we may wish to maintain a minimum insertion delay, as we might have already accounted for a specific minimum clock insertion delay when dividing our design into smaller, more manageable blocks. The transition time is bounded for each technology library. Higher clock transition times can modify the setup of a cell, but we can control this by assuming the largest setup value during static timing analysis. Power is also a concern because of the short-circuit current, but this problem can be addressed by building a tree of clock buffers, i.e., the clock tree. The insertion delay should already have been considered at an earlier stage in the design process by partitioning the design into blocks. The skew remains an open point, and it must be controlled by designing a balanced clock tree. Controlling the skew is important because it affects timing. In particular, a difference between the rising edge at the sending and receiving flip-flops of a sequential design can make the time window available for the combinational delay either larger or shorter, and thus affect timing closure.

As a concluding remark on clock tree synthesis, we want to discuss clock jitter. Clock jitter is a displacement of the clock edge, causing variations in the clock period. Jitter can be caused by an unstable clock source or by temperature and power-supply variations, which makes it relevant for large designs. The occurrence of jitter is unpredictable at design time; we can therefore optimize the circuit for setup and hold by specifying an additional safety margin: the clock uncertainty. The uncertainty is a deterministic reduction of the setup and hold margin that can be used to optimize the circuit for the worst-case scenario. We select an appropriate value in the constraints.sdc file.

Clock Tree Optimization

Clock trees consume substantial (if not the most) power within a design. One reported case stated 40% of total power is consumed by the clock-drivers alone. (See High Performance Processor Design To reduce the clocks switching power, there exists two common methods: - Clock Gating: Conditionally disabling the clock and thus reducing the switching activity. This is done manually in the RTL for maximum benefits, but can also be inferred in synthesis and backend tools. Yosys and OpenROAD have commands that can do this automatically. - Multi-Bit-Flip-Flops (MBFFs): Standard-cells that combine multiple Flip-Flops into a single large cell (usually 2, 4 and 8 Bit), which have to be provided by the standard-cell library.

Routing

Routing connects the standard cells and macros based on the netlist. Finishing ensures the design adheres to manufacturing requirements and is ready for tape-out. Routing Routing is a crucial stage in the physical design flow. It involves:

  1. Global Routing: Planning approximate routes across chip regions to minimize congestion and guide the detailed routing stage.
  2. Optimize Routing: Applying additional optimization steps to resolve timing issues, such as setup or hold violations, and to ensure the design meets performance goals.
  3. Detailed Routing: Finalizing the exact interconnections while adhering to design rules and constraints.

Once routing is complete, the design undergoes finishing, which includes:

  1. Filler Cells: Inserting additional filler cells in areas where standard cells are not placed, ensuring continuous power rails and preventing open areas.
  2. Metal Fill: Adding metal fill patterns to meet manufacturing density requirements.

Finally, the outcome of this exercise will be DEF file, which represents the layout of all the cells and nets in your design. The DEF file can then be converted to a GDS file, the standard format used to represent the final design layout. Eventually, the GDS file will be sent for manufacturing, but first, Design Rule Checking (DRC) and Layout Versus Schematic (LVS) need to be performed

Routing Resources

Before we start routing, we should be familiar with the metal stack, as it plays a significant role in the routing step since it determines the routing resources we have to route our design. The routing direction alternates between neighboring layers and the wires have different dimensions on different layers. Metal stacks are usually designed to have progressively larger wires, which has a reason. Lower metal layers are primarily used for local connections both within standard cells (which are usually just provided as blockages to EDA tools) and between neighboring cells. These layers have narrower wire and smaller spacing, providing higher routing resources to ensure the many connections between standard cells can be routed. In contrast, upper metal layers are more used for global routing and the power grid. These layers have wider wires and larger spacing to reduce parasitics like resistance and capacitance, which is critical for long-distance connections and for carrying power and ground signals. Wider wires in the upper layers help minimize resistance, thereby reducing the IR-drop (voltage drop due to resistance in the power delivery network)

Routing Tracks

Routing tracks serve as a predefined grid on each metal layer that guides the placement of wires. The router engine will later use these tracks to determine where interconnections can be placed. Each track represents a potential wire position, and the track spacing is determined by the pitch of the metal layer.

Power Analysis

Modern CMOS circuits consume power through multiple mechanisms, but the largest contributor in typical digital logic is dynamic power—the energy required to switch logic gates from one state to another. While there are also important sources of static power (mainly due to leakage currents) and short-circuit (or crossover) power, those effects are usually smaller in well-designed CMOS processes, especially under nominal voltages and conservative fabrication technologies.

Sources of Power Dissipation in CMOS

In static CMOS circuits, four main phenomena cause energy dissipation:

  1. Charging and Discharging of Node Capacitances (Dynamic)
  2. Short-Circuit Currents (Dynamic)
  3. Resistive Loads (Static; usually negligible in pure CMOS designs)
  4. Leakage Currents (Static; can become significant in advanced low-voltage processes)

We will not be concerned with static power in this exercise as we limit ourselves to pure CMOS circuits with no resistive loads and because leakage is almost negligible due to the conservative fabrication process being studied. For the needs of EDA tools the dynamic dissipation can be attributed to library cells as follows.

  1. Internal Power: The energy dissipated in the internal nodes of a standard cell due to driving internal transistors and internal node parasitics. This also covers short-circuit currents internal to each gate.
  2. Switching Power: The power associated with charging and discharging the external load, namely the parasitic interconnect capacitances and the input gate capacitances of any cells connected to the output net.

Power Analysis: Generic Flow

Post-layout power analysis is a critical step in the digital design flow. It allows designers to estimate the actual power consumption of an integrated circuit based on its physical implementation, operating conditions, and functional behavior. Accurate power estimation helps assess the design's energy efficiency, ensure thermal reliability, and guide architectural or physical-level optimizations. There are two common methodologies for evaluating dynamic (switching-related) power:

  1. Statistical Power Analysis: Statistical power analysis estimates switching activity based on a fixed average toggle probability, typically assuming uniform and independent switching across all nodes. It is fast and does not require functional simulation, but does not account for actual data patterns or circuit state. It is most useful for early design exploration or as a reference baseline.
  2. Stimuli-Based Power Analysis: Stimuli-based power analysis uses signal activity captured during simulation, usually in the form of a VCD (Value Change Dump) file. The toggle information is mapped onto the post-layout design to compute real switching activity over time. This method is more accurate and reflects realistic circuit behavior under actual workloads or benchmarks, but requires a complete simulation setup and more runtime.

IR Drop

Power analysis provides critical insights into the energy consumption of a circuit, enabling us to identify the most power-hungry components and optimize their design. However, understanding power consumption alone is not enough to ensure reliable operation. The distribution of power across the chip, particularly how voltage levels vary due to current flow and resistive losses, plays a vital role in circuit performance. This phenomenon, known as IR drop, can cause voltage levels to dip below acceptable thresholds, leading to timing failures and degraded functionality.

Physical Verification Design Flow

The goal of the backend design is to convert the structural netlist that we obtained through the front-end design into a physical layout database that essentially contains the geometrical design information for all design layers. This information, also known as mask data, serves as a blueprint for the IC foundry during manufacturing. Essentially the IC foundry gets only a large database that contains a set of geometric shapes (mostly rectangles) in different layers. Some layers in the database are physical, meaning they will be directly fabricated during manufacturing. A good example are the various metal layers used for interconnections. There are some non-physical layers that are used as part of the design flow to identify certain structures (IHP130 uses dedicated layers to identify wire resistor for example), or just for informational purposes (various text layers). The IC foundry defines the design manufacture rules for each of the process layers (so called DRC rules, or design rules). They are hard requirements to ensure the correctness and yield of the manufacturing process. For example, in the IHP130 technology that we use in our exercises, if you want to draw two parallel interconnections on metal-1 (the lowest metal layer), you have to make sure that the metal lines are at least 160nm wide and are separated by at least 180nm between them. DRC is a rigorous process where the entire physical design database is checked against these design rules. Over time, IC manufacturing technology has evolved tremendously. With each technology step, design rules have become more complex and numerous. A modern process has many different manufacturing options, making DRC an increasingly complex operation.

DRC Check

Design Rule Checking (DRC) is a critical verification process in IC design that ensures a chip layout complies with the manufacturing constraints set by the semiconductor foundry. It is performed before a chip is fabricated to prevent design flaws that could lead to manufacturing failures, which is extremely expensive. Each technology node (e.g. 130nm, 65nm, 7nm) has specific design rules that dictate, for example, how close circuit components can be placed, the minimum width of wires, and the required spacing between different layers. A DRC-clean design is less likely to suffer from manufacturing defects such as short circuits and open circuits issues. In VLSI design, manually checking DRC is impractical due to the vast number of wires and transistors in a chip, as well as the extensive set of design rules. Tools like KLayout automate the process by detecting rule violations efficiently, reducing time and effort.

CEP

RTL-to-GDSII Implementation and Optimization of A Digital System

Problem Statement

In this project, students will implement a selected digital system (assigned by the instructor) through a complete RTL-to-GDSII design flow using an appropriate standard-cell technology library and electronic design automation tools. The selected system may be a processor component, controller, datapath, accelerator, communication block, or another synthesizable digital design.

Students must verify the functional correctness of the RTL design, synthesize it into a gate-level netlist, perform physical implementation, and evaluate the final routed design. The physical implementation flow should include floorplanning, power planning, placement, clock-tree synthesis, routing, post-route optimization, timing analysis, and relevant physical-verification checks.

A valid baseline implementation must first be established. Students will then optimize the design with respect to one or two selected metrics from performance, power, and area. Optimization of the selected metrics must not compromise functional correctness or violate the specified timing and physical-design requirements.

Students must compare the baseline and optimized implementations using measurable results and explain the trade-offs introduced by their design and optimization decisions.

Objectives

Upon completing this project, students should be able to:

  1. Apply a complete RTL-to-GDSII flow to a synthesizable digital system.
  2. Verify the functional correctness of the RTL design using an appropriate testbench and simulation environment.
  3. Develop and apply suitable clock, timing, operating-condition, and input/output constraints.
  4. Perform logic synthesis and analyze the resulting gate-level netlist.
  5. Analyze synthesis reports related to timing, power, area, and design correctness.
  6. Perform floorplanning, power planning, placement, clock-tree synthesis, routing, and post-route optimization.
  7. Identify timing, congestion, routing, connectivity, and other physical-design problems using relevant tool reports.
  8. Optimize the implementation for one or two selected metrics from performance, power, and area.
  9. Compare the baseline and optimized implementations using measurable results.
  10. Evaluate and explain the trade-offs between performance, power, and area.
  11. Generate the final layout and verify that it satisfies the required functional, timing, and physical-design conditions.
